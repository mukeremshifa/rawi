# Rawi: learning evidence and a testable tutoring design

Research checked 13 September 2026. This memo separates published findings from proposed product decisions. The founder's audience is UAE college students aged 17–24, including social sciences, business, economics, basic mathematics, physics, biology, and chemistry, plus high-school students aged 13–19. English is primary. Start with an 18+ subset sharing one course; introductory microeconomics is tentative, subject to actual recruitment overlap. Teen expansion needs separate curriculum and user testing.

## What the evidence supports

**Kestin et al. (2025), peer-reviewed college physics RCT.** In a crossover experiment involving 194 Harvard undergraduates and two lessons, a purpose-built AI tutor produced higher immediate post-test scores than an active-learning class. Median learning time was 49 minutes versus 60 classroom minutes; the popular “half the time” formulation is inaccurate. The implementation used expert solutions and a platform that sequenced problem parts. This is evidence for a particular instructional package, with immediate assessments in one course. It does not establish semester-long retention, performance across disciplines, or the effectiveness of an unrestricted chatbot. [Scientific Reports article](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)

**Bastani et al. (2025), peer-reviewed high-school mathematics RCT.** Nearly 1,000 students in Turkey experienced four revision sessions. GPT Base improved assisted practice grades by 48% and guarded GPT Tutor by 127%, relative to control. When AI was removed, GPT Base students scored 17% worse; GPT Tutor largely eliminated this harm without demonstrating a positive unaided effect. These are relative changes, not percentage-point gains. The study distinguishes doing the task with help from acquiring the skill. Its setting and duration limit extrapolation. The August correction changes an author affiliation, not the results. [PNAS article](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2422633122), [correction](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12403119/)

**Tutor CoPilot, November 2025 working-paper version.** This study placed AI suggestions behind human tutors in a K–12 mathematics platform. The revised paper describes more than 700 tutors and 1,000 students; its participant table lists 783 and 1,013. Access increased session exit-ticket mastery by four percentage points, with larger gains for students of lower-rated tutors. The exit ticket was intended to be completed without tutor assistance. End-of-year mathematics scores did not improve significantly, with limited treatment exposure complicating interpretation. It supports human–AI tutoring, not autonomous equivalence. Earlier 900-tutor/1,800-student counts belong to an older version. [Revised paper](https://edworkingpapers.com/sites/default/files/ai24_1054_v2.pdf)

**Chung et al. (2026), Python tutoring preprint.** A five-month course across ten Taipei high schools compared adaptive problem sequencing with a fixed sequence; both arms had an AI tutor. Unaided final-exam performance improved by approximately 0.15 standard deviations. Of 1,047 enrolled students, 770 entered the main analysis after exclusions for missing examinations, cheating, and an accelerated senior track with implementation differences. This is relevant evidence that choosing practice can add value beyond answering questions. It does not establish that reinforcement learning is necessary, or that the result transfers to a voluntary consumer app. Treat it as a preprint with material exclusions. [Author-hosted paper](https://hamsabastani.github.io/llmRL_doe.pdf)

**LearnLM/Eedi, December 2025 exploratory preprint.** The trial involved 165 students aged 13–15 across five UK secondary schools. Human tutors supervised AI messages. The estimated advantage over human-only tutoring on novel problems was 5.5 percentage points, but its 95% credible interval spanned −1.4 to +12.4 points. The point estimate is promising; decisive superiority is not established. Session-level randomization and proximal platform outcomes also limit conclusions about sustained learning or fully autonomous deployment. [Full preprint](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.23633v1)

Three established findings help choose product mechanics. Roediger and Karpicke's experiments with prose learning found better delayed retention after retrieval practice than repeated study, although immediate results and confidence could favor restudy. This supports testing memory beyond the lesson, with limits when generalizing from recall to applied course reasoning. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x)

Cepeda and colleagues studied fact learning in more than 1,350 participants, varying review gaps and final-test delays. The best gap depended on the intended retention interval. This supports spaced review, not a universally optimal “1–3–7 day” schedule. [Original study record](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/psy_facpub/1766/)

Atkinson, Renkl, and Merrill combined progressively removed solution steps with prompts to explain underlying principles. Across two experiments, this improved near and far transfer. Worked examples and fading provide an alternative to requiring a novice to discover every step unaided. [Original article abstract and bibliographic record](https://eric.ed.gov/?id=EJ678596)

The apparent conflict among these studies is useful: intervention design, comparison condition, learner population, and outcome timing differ. We should not pool their headline numbers into a promised Rawi effect. The product hypothesis is that appropriately chosen practice, accurate feedback, and independent checks can create value worth returning for. Rawi must test that hypothesis with its own learners.

## Proposed learning loop

The following is a design proposal, not a validated protocol. Select one course shared by the most reachable students before building its curriculum. A tentative introductory microeconomics unit covers opportunity cost, demand/supply shifts, and elasticity. Give each skill prerequisites, typical misconceptions, worked examples, practice variants, and separate assessment variants. An observable objective is “identify the affected curve and justify the direction of the shift, holding other factors constant.” Do not declare the whole subject mastered after a few activities.

Original, reviewer-authored checks could ask learners to identify the next-best alternative sacrificed, distinguish a curve shift from movement along it, and interpret or calculate elasticity. Specify the percentage-change method, sign convention, units, and rounding in numeric rubrics. Pair calculations with a short explanation so arithmetic success is not mistaken for conceptual understanding. Reference concepts are documented in OpenStax's sections on [opportunity cost](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-3e/pages/2-1-how-individuals-make-choices-based-on-their-budget-constraint), [curve shifts](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-3e/pages/3-2-shifts-in-demand-and-supply-for-goods-and-services?modal=MH), and [elasticity](https://openstax.org/books/principles-microeconomics-3e/pages/5-1-price-elasticity-of-demand-and-price-elasticity-of-supply). These are reference citations, not approved ingestion sources: current pages display restrictions on commercial reuse and GenAI ingestion. Obtain appropriate permission before placing third-party content into the product.

**1. Establish the task and baseline.** The learner chooses a concrete objective and answers two or three short questions. Allow “I do not know yet.” A one-line confidence rating can reveal when confidence and performance diverge. Keep the baseline small enough to start learning quickly; do not imply it precisely estimates ability. Show the scope of the session and let experienced learners skip material after a successful check.

**2. Choose the amount of explanation.** For a new concept, present a compact explanation with one reviewed example. For a learner who already understands it, move directly to practice. Make labeled diagrams or small tables available when useful. Keep explanations adjacent to the relevant graph, equation, or scenario rather than burying them in a long conversation. Offer a plain-language definition for unfamiliar terminology.

**3. Ask for a meaningful attempt.** Use outcome predictions, completing a calculation, identifying an incorrect statement, or explaining a short scenario. A response can be brief: demanding an essay on every turn creates friction unrelated to the learning objective. The learner should be able to say where they are stuck. Do not require arbitrary waiting or repeated incorrect submissions before help appears.

**4. Give targeted feedback and graduated help.** Identify the first relevant misconception, explain why it matters, and ask for the next actionable step. Provide a hint ladder: direct attention to the relevant detail; state the applicable rule; work through a parallel example; then demonstrate the current step when necessary. Record the assistance level. If the learner remains stuck after two substantive hints, offer a worked example or prerequisite refresher instead of continuing an unproductive question loop.

**5. Fade help and vary the problem.** After successful guided practice, remove some scaffolding. Change the values or structure enough to require applying the concept again. Follow a displayed solution with a distinct problem that the learner completes independently. A correct final answer accompanied by an incorrect explanation should trigger a brief probe, not automatic advancement. Conversely, a minor wording difference should not cause an otherwise correct response to fail.

**6. Complete an independent check.** Explicitly enter a short “Check what you can do” mode with fresh items. Pause tutoring hints during the attempt; explain that feedback comes afterward. If the learner exits to request help, allow it and label the attempt as assisted. Use predetermined answers and rubrics wherever possible. Keep the final assessment's answer key out of the conversational tutor's working context.

**7. Return after a delay.** Offer a brief follow-up retrieval check, initially around the next day and a week later. These timings are practical pilot defaults, not claimed optima. Adjust later based on observed forgetting, the target date, and learner preferences. Start each return with a question before revealing the explanation. Show progress as “practiced with help,” “completed independently,” and “remembered later,” so progress reflects the evidence available.

## Learner experience that encourages independence

Make the core screen a problem workspace with a visible objective, response area, help control, and short feedback. Conversation is a support channel within that workspace. Show how the learner's own attempt changed after feedback. End with one useful takeaway and a small next action. Let users resume without rereading the complete transcript.

Use specific encouragement tied to observable work: identifying the relevant assumption or correcting a mistaken interpretation. Avoid inflated praise or permanent labels such as “bad at maths.” Celebrate successful return checks, improvement after a misconception, and appropriate help-seeking. Do not optimize rewards around message volume, continuous sessions, or rapid answer collection.

Explain when an answer was produced from reviewed curriculum and provide a clear “This feedback seems wrong” control. A disputed answer should preserve the learner's work and allow review. If the model is uncertain, it should ask a focused clarification or use a verified example. It must not invent a confident explanation to keep the session moving.

The first release can use reviewed scenarios, simple diagrams, and numeric checks without arbitrary code execution or broad document ingestion. Use familiar UAE student contexts where helpful, with fictional prices clearly labeled. Require sources for factual real-world claims. Course selection should follow shared demand; the Python study above informs instructional design but does not justify choosing programming for this audience.

## Measurement and evaluation plan

Use delayed, independent performance as the primary educational outcome. Record assistance, item identity, skill, attempt count, response, rubric result, curriculum version, and model/prompt version. Store only the data needed for the pilot and analysis. Product retention and learning retention are separate metrics; both matter, and neither substitutes for the other.

Begin with a 10–20-person UAE adult pilot combining observed usability and feasibility checks. Recruit students sharing one course and include people who struggle with the selected material. Check feedback comprehension, recovery from confusion, assistance labeling, completion, cost, and seven-day follow-up. Compare their existing free tool using equivalent tasks and counterbalanced order. This size is an operational choice; it cannot establish reliable educational efficacy. Use [the discovery protocol](../docs/DISCOVERY.md).

Collect a short pre-test, immediate independent post-test, and seven-day independent test. Preregister definitions before reviewing results. Record completion, score variability, follow-up loss, and outside help to inform a later study. Do not market before/after improvement as causal: repeated testing, selection, outside study, and easier post-test items can create misleading gains.

For an efficacy study, randomly assign learners to Rawi or a credible comparison using the same curriculum, practice time allowance, and assessment difficulty. A static worked-example-and-practice experience is a practical initial comparison. Predefine the primary seven-day outcome, analysis, missing-data handling, and minimum effect worth pursuing. Determine sample size from a power calculation after obtaining variance and attrition estimates; an arbitrary “100 users” target is insufficient.

Separate item authorship from tutor tuning. Use parallel forms and counterbalance them; keep assessment items out of examples and prompt-development transcripts. Include near-transfer items and a smaller set of unfamiliar applications. For rubric-scored responses, use reviewers blind to condition and assess agreement. Analyze assigned groups, report attrition and uncertainty, and document external AI use. Remote checks measure independence imperfectly; verify a subset in observed sessions if feasible.

Maintain a second evaluation layer for tutor behavior. Build a reviewed set of correct answers, common misconceptions, ambiguous responses, requests for direct solutions, and multi-turn stuck states. Human reviewers should judge factual correctness, diagnosis, usefulness, appropriate assistance, and correct assessment handling. An LLM judge can triage cases after calibration; it should not be the sole judge of its own pedagogical success.

Proposed pilot release gates are: every published item has a verified answer and rubric; every identified critical correctness defect is resolved; the independent-check path cannot reveal hints before submission; disputed feedback is recoverable; and a curriculum reviewer approves sampled complete sessions. Report failure counts alongside denominators. These are operating standards, not evidence that zero observed errors guarantees future correctness.

Track recurring costs per completed learning loop and per learner who returns for the delayed check. Track latency, abandonment, hint escalation, unassisted success, and delayed success together. An apparent engagement win should be investigated if independent performance declines. Ship additional subjects or personalization complexity only when the current loop works reliably and students voluntarily return to use it.
